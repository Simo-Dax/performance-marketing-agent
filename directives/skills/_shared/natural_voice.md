# Natural voice, the anti-AI-slop rulebook (shared)

Every piece of copy or script this agent produces must sound like a real person, never like an ad reading itself out loud and never like a language model formatting for visual impact. This shared module is the house cadence law for spoken scripts AND written primary text. Every skill that writes spoken or primary text inherits it.

## HARD RULE 1: flowing spoken sentences, not clipped ad-fragments

Real people connect their thoughts with little words: "you just", "and it", "so then", "kind of". Ad copy strips those into telegraphic stacks nobody says out loud. Write the connective tissue back in.

- Bad: "One scoop in water, first thing. Covers your bases, not the whole shelf."
- Good: "You just mix one scoop into water in the morning, and it kind of does what your whole cabinet was trying to."
- Bad: "Lighter. Less bloated. More energy."
- Good: "A couple weeks in, your gut feels better, you're less bloated, and you've actually got energy that lasts."

The ONLY exception is a single deliberate punchline fragment for rhythm ("They smell his presence."), used rarely and on purpose, never to compress an instruction or a benefit.

## HARD RULE 2: the anti-AI pattern filter (run silently before every output)

Before delivering any script or copy, check:

1. Are there stacked one-line statements in a row? If three short sentences appear consecutively, rewrite them into one flowing thought.
2. Is there repeated sentence structure (parallel constructions, matched clause lengths, "Not only... but also...")? Break the symmetry.
3. Does it look formatted for social media instead of spoken naturally? Combine thoughts, let sentences run longer where a person would, and use periods where the reader needs to breathe.

Fix silently. Never announce the filter ran.

## HARD RULE 3: a twelve-year-old has to get it, on the FIRST hearing

Speech is once-through. A reader can go back over a sentence they did not catch; a viewer cannot,
and a viewer who misses a line does not rewind, they scroll. So the bar for spoken copy is HIGHER
than for written, not lower: every line has to land the first time it is heard, for someone about
twelve years old.

That is not a licence to be vague. Simple language is not vaguer language.

- **The keywords always survive.** The product, the category, and the name of the thing that makes
  it work stay in, exactly as they are. You simplify the sentence AROUND them, never them. A
  script smoothed until nobody could say what is being sold has failed this rule, not passed it.
- **Detail only where it earns its place.** One idea per line. A line carrying two ideas gets
  split, or one of them goes.

**When a hard word is allowed, and how to decide.** Do not guess, and do not decide by taste.
Search the VOC for the term:

> If real customers say it, the viewer already knows it and it stays, untouched. If the VOC never
> contains it, it is the BRAND's word rather than the customer's, and it either gets replaced with
> the customer's own word or gets landed in the same breath it is introduced.

"Landed in the same breath" means the sentence carries its own translation: "it's got magnesium
glycinate, which is the kind your body actually absorbs". Name it, then say what it means, inside
one continuous spoken thought. Never name it and move on, and never stop the ad to define it.

With no VOC document on file, the plain word wins by default.

**How to check it.** Do NOT run a reading formula. They are built for prose and they misread
speech, which is why written-copy grade bands are not used here. Do this instead: walk the script
and mark every word a twelve-year-old would not use. For each one, either find it in the VOC, land
it in the same breath, or replace it. A script still carrying an unsourced, unexplained hard word
is not finished.

## The techniques that make it human (USE these)

1. Spoken words, contractions, the register the customer actually uses ("gotta", "that one bro", "drop $500" for a casual brand; calmer but still human for premium).
2. A relatable archetype plus a tiny story, gossipy and specific, like telling a friend about someone you both know.
3. Concrete everyday specifics: named places, named amounts, tossed off casually. Specifics beat abstractions every time.
4. Talk TO one person, casual second person.
5. Spoken emphasis where a voice would stress a word (one CAPS word maximum).
6. Casual real numbers and name-drops, only sourced ones.
7. A cheeky, slightly contrarian aside.
8. Plain mechanism, no buzzwords: explain the how the way a friend would.
9. Voice the customer's own objection in their own words, then disarm it.

## AI-slop tells (BAN these)

- "Imagine..." / "Picture this..." / "What if..." openers.
- Parallel listy structure: "The probiotics... The adaptogens...", "Not only... but also...".
- Clipped telegraphic comma-stacks that drop the words speech uses.
- Writerly phrases nobody says out loud: "quietly sure", "a testament to", "in a world where", "say goodbye to", "look no further", "the one thing that matters".
- Long dashes used for literary rhythm, perfectly balanced clauses, zero contractions. This plugin never uses em dashes anywhere; use commas, "and", or a new sentence.
- Abstract benefit-speak instead of a concrete picture ("supports your wellness journey").
- Corporate words: elevate, optimize, unlock, premium experience, foundational, transform, seamless, effortless, game-changer, revolutionize.
- Hype punctuation: exclamation marks, emoji.
- Fake conversational tics inserted to seem human ("Look,", "Here's the thing:" as every second opener).

## The test

Read every line OUT LOUD. If you would never say it to a friend at a kitchen table, if it sounds like an announcer or a brochure, rewrite it. Match the brand's register (a frat-bro brand and a premium wellness brand sound like different people), but BOTH must sound like a real human, just a different real human. Casual is not sloppy: it is still tight, specific, and ends on the CTA.

## Written copy addendum (primary text and landing copy)

Everything above applies to written copy with one adjustment: written copy tolerates slightly tighter sentences than speech, but the read-aloud test still decides. If the primary text reads like a press release or a listicle, it fails. The first 125 characters especially must sound like a person mid-thought, because that is the line that has to stop a thumb.
